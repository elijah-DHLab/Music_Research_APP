Three rules first:
  1. Never add Spotify streams to YouTube views. They come from different audiences and use different units.
  2. Missing data is not zero. A song absent from the chart may still have had listeners.
  3. These are Top-N charts, not a sample of all listening in Israel.

3. Working with the chart

Filtering and selection

The filter bar at the top (source, language, gender, genre, dates, rank, search) applies to all tabs. ↺ Reset everything at the end of the bar restores the initial state: filters, selected series, zoom and periods.

Above the chart, choose a group by dimension (e.g. language), a secondary split (e.g. source), a measure, a chart type, a normalization and a time unit. Use the series picker below the chart to compare two, three or more series.

Zooming and navigating time

  • Drag horizontally on the chart to zoom into that range. If “Drag to create comparison period” is checked, dragging defines a period instead.
  • ＋ and － zoom in and out step by step. ↶ Previous range goes back one step.
  • Double-click the chart, or use Show full timeline, to return to the full range.
  • ⛶ Full screen enlarges the chart to the whole screen. In full screen, the mouse wheel zooms around the cursor. Outside full screen, use Ctrl+wheel. Press Esc to exit.

Reading values

  • Hover over a line to see the series, date and value. Hover over a dashed line to see the event name.
  • Click a point to see which songs, artists and languages make up that value in that time bucket.
  • Click a table row to pin a series (★). Click a legend item to hide or show it. “Raw” opens the source rows.
  • The i button next to the chart title explains how the current view is calculated and what its coverage is.

4. War events on the chart

Each event is marked with a dashed line and a number at the top of the chart. The color shows the event type: war & operations, hostages & agreements, losses & disasters, regional escalation, political developments, society & commemoration. Below the chart, a numbered list shows the events in the visible range. Click an event in the list to zoom to six weeks before and after it.

  • ⚑ Events shows or hides the event lines.
  • Event names shows or hides the names on the chart. Where events are too close together, only the number appears on the chart, and the name is in the list below it. Zooming in separates crowded events.
  • In the Events tab you can choose which event types to show and add your own events. Events you add are saved only in your browser.

An event that coincides with a change in the chart does not prove that it caused the change. Compare with similar periods without an event.

5. Comparing sources and normalization

  • Share within each source (%) — recommended. Each value is a percentage of all streams or chart appearances in the same source and time bucket. When sources are combined, the average uses only the sources actually observed in that bucket. Buckets with partial coverage are hatched.
  • Absolute numbers — suitable only within a single source. When showing several sources, split by source.
  • Index — each series relative to its mean in the first period (=100). Use it to compare trends, not amounts.
  • Logarithmic — a log axis for raw values.

The common time unit is the Thursday-aligned week, as in the YouTube charts. Spotify days are grouped into the matching week. For a controlled cross-source comparison, choose “Split by: Source” so that each source gets its own line.

6. Period comparison

The default periods include “Before the war” and “First weeks”. You can rename periods, edit their dates, add new ones (or drag on the chart when that option is checked) and reset them. For each dimension, the tab shows the share in each period, the change between periods, and the songs and artists that changed most. The top filters apply here as well. Periods are saved in your browser, and the comparison can be exported to CSV.

7. Songs, performances and artists

The model has three levels: a work (the song as a composition), a performance/recording (a specific version by a specific performer) and a chart observation (that recording in a given source, date and rank). The same performance can appear on Spotify and YouTube under different titles, so it is unified under a performance ID.

What normalization did

  • Removed invisible characters, Hebrew vowel marks and direction marks. Name matching ignores letter case and punctuation.
  • Removed additions such as “(Official Video)”, “(קליפ רשמי)” and production credits from YouTube titles, as well as a leading performer name.
  • Channel names (such as “Kan Gimel” or “Topic”) are not treated as artists.
  • Bilingual names of the same performer were merged only where there was evidence for it.
  • Identical titles by different performers were not merged (e.g. Daylight by David Kushner and by Drake). Live, Remix, Acoustic and Cover versions remain separate performances and are marked in the “Version” dimension.

V2.7 gap-fill

Of 895 recordings first seen in the gap-filled data: 286 were joined to an existing performance, 9 duplicates were merged, 472 were linked to a known performer and 128 remain unmatched. All are flagged for review (needs_review). Of 188 new performers, 88 were enriched automatically from Wikidata and 94 were not found (mostly Arabic-language performers). Performance identities are provisional until manually verified.

8. Interpretive cautions

  • Charts reflect the composition of the Top-N, not total listening volume or listeners’ motivations.
  • Spotify and YouTube represent different audiences. Differences between Tracks and Videos may reflect how YouTube classifies content rather than the music itself.
  • Language, genre and gender are proxies. Genre belongs to the performer, and gender refers to the tagged performer, not the audience.
  • Each song’s first week on the chart counts as a “new entry”, so this measure is inflated at the start of the period.
  • The numbers in the Research questions tab are preliminary descriptive calculations. Validate them against coverage and performance identities before publication.
